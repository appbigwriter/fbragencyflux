# Daily Mobility Routine for Lower Back & Hips After 40

**Category:** Strength & Movement / Mobility  
**Last reviewed:** September 26, 2026

> **Short answer:** A useful mobility routine is gentle, repeatable, and adapted to your body. This sequence uses breathing, controlled range of motion, and light movement—not forceful stretching or a promise to “fix” back pain.

## Before you begin

This is general education, not physical therapy or individualized medical advice. Stop if movement causes sharp, escalating, radiating, or unusual pain. Seek urgent care for back pain with new bladder or bowel changes, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or significant weakness/numbness in both legs.

If you have had surgery, a recent injury, osteoporosis, inflammatory disease, neurological symptoms, or persistent pain, ask a qualified clinician or physiotherapist for modifications first.

## The 10-minute routine

Move slowly and breathe normally. Stay in a range that feels manageable; mobility work should not be a test of toughness.

### 1. Supported breathing and posture — 1 minute

Sit or lie comfortably. Let your ribs expand as you inhale and soften as you exhale. Notice whether you are gripping your lower back, shoulders, or jaw. The goal is to reduce unnecessary bracing, not to force a “perfect” posture.

### 2. Pelvic tilts — 8 slow repetitions

Lie on your back with knees bent and feet supported, or perform the movement seated. Gently alternate between a neutral pelvis and a small flattening/arching motion. Keep it comfortable and controlled.

### 3. Knee rocks — 8 repetitions per side

On your back with knees bent, let both knees move a small distance to one side, then return to center and switch. Keep the range small enough that your shoulders remain relaxed.

### 4. Cat-cow variation — 6 to 8 repetitions

On hands and knees, or with hands supported on a counter, gently round and lengthen your spine. Move with your breath and avoid collapsing into a painful range. A supported standing version works well if getting onto the floor is uncomfortable.

### 5. Hip hinge practice — 8 repetitions

Stand with feet comfortable apart and hands on your hips. Soften your knees, send your hips back a little, then return to standing. Keep your spine long and the range modest. This rehearses a useful movement pattern for lifting and reaching.

### 6. Half-kneeling hip shift — 5 gentle repetitions per side

Use a chair or wall for support. Place one foot forward and the opposite knee down on a cushion, or use a standing split stance. Shift your body slightly forward while keeping ribs stacked over the pelvis. Do not force the stretch or arch your lower back.

### 7. Supported side bend and reach — 4 repetitions per side

Stand near a wall or chair. Reach one arm overhead and make a small side bend, then return. Keep the movement smooth and avoid twisting into pain.

### 8. Easy walk — 2 minutes

Finish with comfortable walking around the room or outside. Gradual general activity often matters more than a dramatic stretch session.

## How often to do it

Start with five to ten minutes on most days, or use selected movements as a warm-up before a walk or strength session. NIA guidance encourages combining aerobic, muscle-strengthening, balance, and flexibility activities across the week. Increase duration or range gradually, not both at once.

A little stiffness that settles as you move can be different from pain that worsens, spreads, or changes your function. Track how you feel during and after the routine rather than chasing an intense stretch sensation.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a mobility routine as a diagnosis or cure.
- Stretching through sharp, electric, or radiating pain.
- Copying a range of motion from someone with a different body or history.
- Holding your breath or forcing end ranges.
- Assuming every lower-back symptom is caused by tight hips.
- Ignoring persistent pain or neurological warning signs.

## When to seek care

Contact a qualified professional for persistent or recurrent pain, symptoms after a significant fall or injury, unexplained fever or weight loss, night pain, progressive weakness, numbness, or difficulty walking. Seek urgent care for new bladder/bowel control changes, numbness around the saddle area, or severe weakness/numbness in both legs.

## Takeaway

The best daily mobility routine is gentle enough to repeat and specific enough to monitor. Use the sequence as a starting point, adapt it to your context, and treat worsening or unusual symptoms as a reason to seek assessment—not as a challenge to stretch harder.
