# Protein Intake for Active Adults 40+: A Practical Guide

**Category:** Strength & Movement / Nutrition Evidence  
**Last reviewed:** September 28, 2026

> **Short answer:** Protein supports muscle repair and maintenance, but more is not automatically better. For many healthy older adults, expert guidance commonly discusses roughly 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day, with higher needs possible in some situations. Kidney disease, liver disease, pregnancy, medication, and medical nutrition needs require individualized advice.

## Why protein matters after 40

Muscle responds to resistance training and dietary protein throughout adulthood. With age, some people experience “anabolic resistance,” meaning a larger or better-timed stimulus may be useful for maintaining muscle. That does not turn protein into a shortcut: progressive training, adequate energy, sleep, and overall dietary quality still matter.

## How much is enough?

Protein targets are not universal prescriptions. Reviews and expert groups have suggested at least about **1.0–1.2 grams per kilogram of body weight per day** for many healthy adults over 65, with context-dependent adjustments for training, frailty, illness, energy intake, and goals.

For an active 75 kg adult, that range would correspond to an estimated 75–90 g/day. This is an illustration, not a recommendation for a specific person. Using current body weight may also be inappropriate in some clinical situations; a registered dietitian or clinician can choose a better basis.

Avoid turning the number into a pass/fail score. A person eating less protein than a target is not “failing,” and a higher number is not automatically beneficial.

## Spread protein across meals

Instead of trying to fit all protein into dinner, include a meaningful source at breakfast, lunch, dinner, and—if useful—a snack. Spreading intake can make the target easier to reach and pairs naturally with regular meals.

Useful food sources include:

- eggs, dairy, Greek yogurt, and cottage cheese;
- fish, poultry, lean meats, and seafood;
- tofu, tempeh, edamame, beans, lentils, and peas;
- nuts, seeds, and whole grains as complementary sources.

Plant-based patterns can provide adequate protein when meals are varied and total intake is sufficient. Dietary preference, culture, budget, appetite, and tolerability matter.

## Food first, supplements when practical

A protein powder can be convenient when appetite, time, travel, or chewing difficulty makes food-based intake hard. It is not inherently superior to protein-rich food. Check the label for protein amount, added stimulants, allergens, sweeteners, and independent quality-testing signals when available.

Do not use protein products as a replacement for a varied diet, and do not assume a “mass gainer” or “lean muscle” label proves a predictable result.

## Protein and resistance training work as a system

Protein supplementation combined with resistance training may improve muscle mass and strength in older adults compared with training alone, but studies vary. A useful weekly system includes:

1. resistance training for major muscle groups;
2. protein-containing meals distributed through the day;
3. adequate calories and fluids;
4. recovery and sleep;
5. gradual progression rather than punishment.

Protein cannot compensate for an absent training stimulus, severe energy restriction, or untreated illness.

## When more protein may not be appropriate

Ask a qualified clinician or registered dietitian before significantly increasing protein if you have chronic kidney disease, liver disease, heart failure, diabetes requiring individualized nutrition, a history of kidney stones, gastrointestinal disease, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take medications affected by dietary changes.

Unexplained weight loss, weakness, persistent appetite loss, difficulty swallowing, or rapid functional decline deserve medical assessment rather than a supplement purchase.

## Common myths

- **“High protein automatically damages healthy kidneys.”** Evidence and risk differ between healthy people and people with kidney disease; individual context matters.
- **“Protein makes you bulky.”** Muscle gain depends on training, energy balance, genetics, time, and many other factors.
- **“Only animal protein counts.”** Plant proteins can contribute meaningfully within a varied diet.
- **“A shake replaces strength training.”** It does not.
- **“More is always better.”** Excess can add cost, calories, digestive discomfort, and unnecessary restriction.

## Takeaway

Build meals around a practical protein source, distribute intake, train progressively, and seek individualized guidance when health conditions affect nutrition. The useful goal is adequate, sustainable nourishment—not the highest number on a label.
