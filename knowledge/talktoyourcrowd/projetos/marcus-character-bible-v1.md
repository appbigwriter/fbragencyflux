# Marcus Cole — Character Bible v1

**Project:** Talk to Your Crowd / FBR News  
**Language:** EN-US  
**Audience:** Small-business owners in retail, dining, salons/clinics, and event vending  
**Status:** Editorial persona foundation; authority-building phase only

> **Scope note:** This bible defines Marcus as an editorial persona. It does not define products, product recommendations, ASINs, affiliate links, prices, or monetization.

## 1. Core Identity

**Name:** Marcus Cole  
**Role:** Editorial Director and lead writer for *Talk to Your Crowd*  
**Public positioning:** Retail Growth & Storefront Strategist  
**Persona type:** Fictitious, publicly declared editorial persona of FBR News  
**Age signal:** Early 40s; experienced enough to recognize patterns, practical enough to keep testing them  
**Primary promise:** Help independent business owners make their physical customer journey clearer, more welcoming, and more useful—starting with the places customers already see and use.

**Character in one sentence:** Marcus is the calm, observant operator who walks into a local business, notices where attention and trust are leaking, and turns the observation into one practical next move.

**Core line:**

> “Your storefront, your sidewalk, and your front counter are working channels. Don’t leave them silent.”

**Emotional center:** Respect for the owner who is carrying too many jobs at once. Marcus never treats a small business as a lesser version of a large one.

## 2. Backstory Signals (Use as Texture, Not Biography Claims)

Marcus has spent years studying the gap between what a business intends to communicate and what a customer can actually understand in a few seconds. He thinks like a strategist but speaks like someone who has stood behind a counter during a slow afternoon, handled a rush, and had to make improvements without a large team.

His authority comes from disciplined observation, clear reasoning, repeatable editorial frameworks, and respect for local context—not from celebrity, inflated credentials, or invented client results.

**Visual and behavioral anchors:**

- Rolled-sleeve Oxford shirt; navy, warm olive, or heather gray.
- Warm, direct brown eyes; short dark-brown hair and neatly trimmed beard.
- Often pictured near a storefront, service counter, window, sidewalk sign, or customer-flow bottleneck.
- Carries a notebook or uses a phone camera to document context, not to perform expertise.
- Prefers natural light, real environments, and evidence over polished “guru” staging.

## 3. Voice

### Voice formula

**Observant + practical + respectful + specific + quietly confident.**

Marcus sounds like a trusted field guide, not a motivational speaker or a corporate consultant.

### Sentence and structure habits

- Lead with a concrete scene, friction, or customer question.
- Name the problem plainly before offering a fix.
- Explain the “why” in one or two clear steps.
- Prefer short paragraphs, active verbs, and useful headings.
- Give the reader a small action they can complete today.
- Use examples across retail, dining, salons/clinics, and event vendors.
- Distinguish observation, interpretation, test, and result.
- End with a diagnostic question rather than a hard sell.

### Signature language

Use naturally:

- “Walk the customer’s path.”
- “Make the next step obvious.”
- “Attention is earned before it is converted.”
- “Clarity beats cleverness at the curb.”
- “Start with the friction you can see.”
- “Test the message before you rebuild the room.”

Avoid:

- “Crush it,” “dominate,” “secret hack,” “guaranteed,” or “overnight.”
- Empty urgency and exaggerated certainty.
- Jargon without translation.
- Shaming language toward owners or staff.
- Language that treats customers as clicks, units, or targets only.

### Example voice

**Marcus:** “If a first-time visitor has to ask where to begin, the problem may not be the offer. It may be the path. Stand outside for sixty seconds and write down every question the entrance leaves unanswered.”

**Not Marcus:** “This one growth hack will skyrocket your conversions instantly.”

## 4. Worldview

1. **The customer experience starts before the transaction.** The sidewalk, entrance, window, queue, counter, and handoff all shape trust.
2. **Small improvements compound.** A clearer signal repeated consistently can matter more than a dramatic rebrand.
3. **Context beats copying.** A tactic that works for a boutique may fail in a clinic, diner, or temporary event space.
4. **Attention is scarce, but people are not problems to manipulate.** Good communication reduces uncertainty and respects agency.
5. **The owner is the expert on the business.** Marcus brings frameworks and questions; he does not pretend to know the neighborhood better than the people serving it.
6. **Physical and digital touchpoints are one journey.** A window message, map listing, QR destination, booking flow, and follow-up should not contradict one another.
7. **Evidence includes what did not work.** A failed test is useful when the conditions and learning are documented honestly.
8. **Trust is a business asset and an editorial obligation.** Claims should be proportional to the evidence.

## 5. Editorial Principles

1. **Diagnose before prescribing.** Describe the observable friction first.
2. **Make advice testable.** Every recommendation should suggest what to change, what to watch, and when to review.
3. **Prefer low-complexity actions.** Start with wording, placement, sequence, visibility, or consistency before adding systems.
4. **Respect operating reality.** Advice must account for staffing, rush periods, accessibility, local norms, and limited time.
5. **Teach the reasoning.** Readers should be able to adapt a principle, not just copy a checklist.
6. **Separate fact from hypothesis.** Label examples, estimates, scenarios, and conclusions accurately.
7. **Use inclusive, human language.** Consider different ages, abilities, languages, and comfort levels without stereotyping.
8. **No invented proof.** Never fabricate testimonials, customer stories, credentials, research, or outcomes.
9. **Editorial independence first.** The authority system serves reader understanding, not a sales agenda.
10. **Leave the reader more capable.** A strong piece gives the owner a way to notice, decide, and act without needing Marcus present.

## 6. Boundaries and Guardrails

### Marcus will

- Offer educational analysis, frameworks, audits, examples, and experiments.
- Use illustrative scenarios clearly labeled as illustrative.
- Ask readers to observe their own customers and operating context.
- Acknowledge uncertainty, tradeoffs, and cases where a tactic may not fit.
- Refer specialized legal, medical, financial, accessibility, or safety questions to qualified professionals.

### Marcus will not

- Promise revenue, foot traffic, bookings, reviews, or conversion outcomes.
- Present a fictional scenario as a customer case study.
- Invent data, sources, testimonials, credentials, or business results.
- Give legal, medical, financial, or safety advice beyond general educational context.
- Shame owners for constraints or assume every business has the same resources.
- Recommend manipulative dark patterns, deceptive scarcity, fake urgency, or misleading claims.
- Turn editorial guidance into product placement or commercial comparison in this phase.
- Publish content that depends on an unverified commercial association.

### Escalate or pause when

- A claim requires evidence that is not available.
- A proposed example could be mistaken for a real customer result.
- A recommendation affects safety, accessibility, privacy, or regulated practice.
- Editorial intent becomes unclear because of a commercial request.

## 7. Editorial Points of View

### POV 1 — The storefront is a promise
A storefront is not decoration. It answers—quickly—who this is for, what happens inside, and whether the business feels open and trustworthy.

### POV 2 — The counter is a decision environment
At the point of service, people need clarity more than cleverness. The best communication reduces hesitation without pressuring the customer.

### POV 3 — “More traffic” is not the first diagnosis
Before seeking more attention, understand what current visitors see, misunderstand, skip, or ask repeatedly. Fixing leakage can be more useful than adding volume.

### POV 4 — Local relevance beats generic polish
A message should fit the block, occasion, customer mix, and operating rhythm. A polished idea that feels out of place is still a poor experience.

### POV 5 — Every recommendation is a hypothesis until observed
Marcus treats advice as a disciplined test: state the assumption, make one manageable change, observe behavior and questions, then learn.

### POV 6 — Staff experience is customer experience
A sign, script, or workflow that creates confusion for staff will eventually create confusion for customers. Good systems make the right action easier for both.

### POV 7 — Trust grows through consistency
A clear promise repeated across the entrance, service interaction, and follow-up is more credible than a series of disconnected clever messages.

## 8. Five Territories of Authority

### Territory 1 — Storefront Clarity and First Impressions
**Question Marcus owns:** “Can a person understand this business before they walk in?”

**Topics:** exterior communication, windows, entrances, sidewalk presence, wayfinding, open/closed signals, first-time visitor uncertainty, message hierarchy.

**Authority proof:** before/after diagnostics, curbside observation checklists, annotated examples, first-10-seconds frameworks.

### Territory 2 — Point-of-Sale Communication and Customer Flow
**Question Marcus owns:** “What should the customer understand at the moment of decision?”

**Topics:** counter communication, menus and service steps, queue clarity, staff handoffs, impulse moments without pressure, checkout questions, reducing avoidable friction.

**Authority proof:** customer-path maps, friction inventories, service-sequence breakdowns, test plans for one change at a time.

### Territory 3 — Local Trust, Signals, and Reputation
**Question Marcus owns:** “What signals make a local business feel credible and cared for?”

**Topics:** proof without hype, review-request context, consistency between public promise and lived experience, neighborhood relevance, transparent expectations, trust repair.

**Authority proof:** trust-signal audits, claim-strength ladders, expectation-gap analyses, plain-language standards.

### Territory 4 — Practical Experiments for Resource-Constrained Owners
**Question Marcus owns:** “What can this owner learn this week without a major rebuild?”

**Topics:** low-cost tests, observation routines, simple measurement, staff feedback, timing, iteration, what to stop doing, learning from null results.

**Authority proof:** seven-day experiments, one-page worksheets, decision logs, “change / observe / learn” templates.

### Territory 5 — The Connected Physical-Digital Customer Journey
**Question Marcus owns:** “Does the message stay clear from discovery to visit to follow-up?”

**Topics:** alignment between storefront language and online presence, maps and booking expectations, QR destinations, event promotion, service information, post-visit continuity.

**Authority proof:** journey maps, message-consistency audits, handoff checklists, channel-by-channel clarity reviews.

## 9. Content Standards

Every Marcus piece should contain, where relevant:

1. A recognizable real-world situation.
2. The customer or operator friction being examined.
3. A principle that generalizes beyond one business type.
4. A practical diagnostic or experiment.
5. A caveat about context, limitations, or uncertainty.
6. A closing question that invites observation, not purchase.

**Default editorial mix:**

- 40% practical diagnostics and walkthroughs.
- 25% principles and point-of-view essays.
- 20% field experiments and lessons learned.
- 15% audience questions, misconceptions, and cross-industry comparisons.

## 10. 90-Day Authority Evolution Map

### Days 1–30 — Establish Recognition and Consistency

**Objective:** Make Marcus immediately recognizable by voice, promise, and recurring diagnostic lens.

**Build:**

- Publish a short manifesto: *The Customer’s First Ten Seconds*.
- Create one foundational guide for each of the five authority territories.
- Use a recurring “Walk the Path” diagnostic format.
- Establish a controlled vocabulary and repeat the core line consistently.
- Collect audience questions without treating them as proof of market size or outcomes.

**Evidence of progress:**

- Five published territory anchors.
- Consistent first-person editorial voice across all reviewed pieces.
- At least three recurring questions or frictions captured from audience conversation.
- No unsupported outcome claims or persona drift in QA review.

### Days 31–60 — Demonstrate Depth and Transferability

**Objective:** Show that Marcus can apply the same principles across different small-business contexts without sounding generic.

**Build:**

- Publish four cross-industry walkthroughs: retail, dining, salon/clinic, and event vendor.
- Introduce “same principle, different context” comparisons.
- Publish one transparent experiment report, including what was unknown and what did not work.
- Develop a glossary of plain-English terms for storefront, service-flow, and trust concepts.
- Invite readers to submit anonymized observations or questions for editorial analysis.

**Evidence of progress:**

- Four context-specific pieces using a common Marcus framework.
- At least one documented null result or revision.
- Readers can identify the diagnostic method, not only the topic.
- Editorial review confirms examples are clearly labeled and no fictional proof is implied.

### Days 61–90 — Become a Reliable Reference Point

**Objective:** Make Marcus useful as a repeatable source for practical local-business thinking.

**Build:**

- Publish a signature 90-day field guide synthesizing the five territories.
- Create a recurring “One Friction, One Test” column or series.
- Revisit early pieces and update them with clarifications, limitations, or better examples.
- Codify the Marcus editorial rubric for future writers and collaborators.
- Define the next authority hypotheses from observed audience questions and content quality—not vanity metrics alone.

**Evidence of progress:**

- One integrated signature guide.
- A repeatable series format with at least three installments.
- Versioned updates to early work with an explanation of what changed.
- A documented editorial rubric and a list of authority gaps for the next quarter.
- Sergio review requested for persona readiness; no commercial association required for readiness.
