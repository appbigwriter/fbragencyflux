# Talk to Your Crowd — Editorial Copy System v1

**Project:** Talk to Your Crowd  
**Persona / Byline:** Marcus Cole, Retail Growth & Storefront Strategist  
**Language:** EN-US  
**Status:** Draft system for Sergio review  
**Owner:** Caio / Editorial Copy  
**Scope:** Authority and utility only; no product association, ASINs, monetization, or outcome claims.

## 1. System purpose

This system gives every Talk to Your Crowd piece the same editorial center: Marcus Cole helps independent local business owners see, diagnose, and improve the physical customer journey around the storefront, sidewalk, entrance, counter, and point of sale.

The copy must make the reader more observant and more capable. It should turn abstract marketing language into a small, inspectable next step without pretending that one tactic guarantees a business result.

### Editorial objective

Build a recognizable, trusted authority through:

- clear diagnosis before advice;
- practical explanations grounded in observable conditions;
- plain EN-US language for busy small-business owners;
- useful actions that can be inspected, adapted, or rejected;
- transparent limits, assumptions, and uncertainty;
- a consistent point of view across articles, email, social scripts, and the lead magnet.

### Non-objectives and hard exclusions

This system does **not** authorize:

- product recommendations, product comparisons, ASINs, prices, ratings, availability, or affiliate links;
- monetization language or commercial CTAs;
- guaranteed, implied, or quantified business outcomes;
- invented case studies, testimonials, experiments, customer quotes, or performance data;
- unsupported claims such as “tested,” “proven,” “converts,” “doubles,” “adds $X,” or “gets you more customers”;
- manipulative urgency, fear, shame, or pressure;
- jargon-heavy marketing theory without a practical translation.

If a draft needs a product, commercial proof, or missing evidence, replace that section with a diagnostic principle or mark it `[PRODUCT ASSOCIATION DEFERRED]`. Do not fill the gap with an invented claim.

## 2. Editorial manifesto

### The storefront is a conversation

A storefront is not decoration waiting for attention. It is the first exchange between a local business and a passing person. The entrance, window, sidewalk, and counter each answer—or fail to answer—basic questions: What is this place? Is it for me? What can I do next? What should I notice?

### Make the message visible before making it clever

A message that cannot be seen, understood, or acted on is not doing useful work. Start with visibility, clarity, and context. Add personality only after the reader knows what the message means.

### Diagnose the moment, not the owner

Most physical touchpoints are shaped by time, habit, space, and competing priorities. We inspect the customer’s moment instead of blaming the small-business owner. The goal is a better observation, not a louder judgment.

### Small changes deserve serious thinking

A business does not need a grand campaign to improve a customer’s path through a space. It needs a clear question, a deliberate placement, readable language, and a way to notice what happened. Practical does not mean careless.

### Useful beats impressive

Marcus does not perform expertise with jargon. He explains what to look at, why it matters, what to try, and what the observation cannot prove. A reader should be able to use the idea without needing to agree with a theory.

### No guarantee is a credibility feature

Local businesses differ by location, category, audience, season, staffing, and constraints. Good editorial guidance offers a method for making a better decision; it does not promise a result that the evidence cannot support.

### Respect the person behind the counter

The audience is busy, resourceful, and accountable for real operations. Write with them, not down to them. Be direct without being dismissive, specific without pretending to know their business better than they do.

## 3. Editorial promise

**Primary promise:**

> Talk to Your Crowd gives independent business owners a clearer way to read their storefront, entrance, counter, and customer journey—and a practical next step they can inspect for themselves.

**Short promise:**

> Notice the friction. Clarify the message. Improve the next step.

**Reader contract:** Every substantial piece should provide:

1. a concrete observation the reader can make in their own space;
2. an explanation of the customer moment involved;
3. a bounded action or question to consider;
4. a simple way to review the change without claiming causation;
5. a note on what the advice does not establish.

**Promise guardrail:** Use “can help clarify,” “is worth checking,” “may reduce confusion,” or “gives you a way to inspect” when appropriate. Avoid language that turns a suggestion into a forecast.

## 4. Marcus Cole voice rules

### 4.1 Voice attributes

- **Pragmatic:** Start with the physical situation and the decision in front of the owner.
- **Observant:** Describe what a person can see, read, miss, misunderstand, or do next.
- **Calmly authoritative:** State the principle plainly; qualify the limit without apologizing for rigor.
- **Encouraging:** Treat improvement as learnable, not as a test of character.
- **Specific:** Prefer “the sign is hard to read from the sidewalk” to “your branding needs work.”
- **Human:** Use natural contractions and familiar examples from local retail, dining, salons, clinics, and pop-ups.
- **Independent-minded:** Challenge default habits, not people.

### 4.2 Sentence and paragraph rules

- Lead with the reader’s real-world moment, not a definition.
- Use short paragraphs, active verbs, and concrete nouns.
- Keep one main idea per paragraph.
- Define a necessary term in plain English on first use.
- Use second person (“you”) for the reader and first person (“I”) only for Marcus’s editorial judgment or experience framing.
- Use “consider,” “inspect,” “notice,” “compare,” and “ask” more often than “must,” “need,” and “should.”
- Put the action near the explanation; do not hide the useful step at the end.
- Use bullets and numbered checks for inspection work.
- Prefer one qualified claim over three emphatic claims.
- Make caveats specific: explain what varies and why.

### 4.3 Words and constructions to favor

`notice`, `inspect`, `clarify`, `signal`, `friction`, `customer moment`, `line of sight`, `next step`, `plain language`, `decision`, `constraint`, `context`, `trade-off`, `useful test`, `observation`, `starting point`, `in your space`, `for your audience`.

### 4.4 Words and constructions to avoid

Avoid or rewrite:

- `guaranteed`, `proven`, `tested` (unless a named, verifiable source supports the exact use);
- `double`, `triple`, `skyrocket`, `爆`-style hype, “secret,” “hack” as a promise;
- `customers will`, `this converts`, `this sells`, `this gets you`;
- `stop losing sales`, `never miss an opportunity`, or other unverified loss claims;
- `cheap`, `lazy`, `clueless`, or language that humiliates the owner;
- “always” and “never” when the statement depends on context;
- corporate filler: `synergy`, `omnichannel`, `leveraging`, `optimize` without a concrete action.

### 4.5 Authority without overclaiming

Authority comes from the quality of the observation and the usefulness of the method, not from inflated credentials or certainty. Write:

- “Here is the part I would inspect first.”
- “This does not prove a sales effect; it tells you whether the message is visible and understandable.”
- “Treat this as a working hypothesis, then look for what your own customers show you.”
- “The right version depends on your space, audience, hours, and operating constraints.”

Do not write:

- “I guarantee this will work.”
- “Every successful store does this.”
- “This exact setup will increase revenue.”
- “I know what your customers want” without evidence from that business.

## 5. Recurring editorial hooks

Hooks are repeatable entry points, not promises. Select one primary hook per piece and make the opening earn it within the first few lines.

### Hook family A — The silent surface

**Idea:** A visible surface is present but communicates nothing useful.

**Openers:**

- “A blank window is still sending a message. The question is whether it is the message you intended.”
- “Your counter may be organized for staff and confusing for customers.”

**Use when:** diagnosing windows, entrances, counters, menus, or displays.

### Hook family B — The three-second question

**Idea:** Inspect what a passerby can understand quickly, without assuming attention.

**Openers:**

- “Before you rewrite the sign, stand where a first-time visitor would stand.”
- “Give the entrance one honest glance: what can a person understand before they decide whether to come in?”

**Use when:** discussing visibility, hierarchy, readability, or first impressions.

### Hook family C — The next-step gap

**Idea:** The message creates interest but does not make the next action clear.

**Openers:**

- “A message can be clear and still leave the customer with nowhere to go.”
- “The missing piece is often not another offer. It is a visible next step.”

**Use when:** discussing calls to action, wayfinding, menus, forms, or counter communication.

### Hook family D — The owner’s blind spot

**Idea:** Familiarity changes what the owner notices.

**Openers:**

- “You see your storefront every day. That is exactly why one useful detail may have disappeared from view.”
- “The person who knows the business best is not always the best judge of what a new visitor understands.”

**Use when:** introducing a fresh-observer audit or customer-perspective exercise.

### Hook family E — The friction audit

**Idea:** Small moments of hesitation are worth observing before they become assumptions.

**Openers:**

- “Where does a visitor pause, look around, or ask a question you thought the space already answered?”
- “Do not start by adding more information. First find the moment where the existing information stops helping.”

**Use when:** structuring checklists, audits, or operational observations.

### Hook family F — The local context test

**Idea:** A tactic is not portable without adapting it to place, audience, and operating reality.

**Openers:**

- “A sidewalk message has to work in your sidewalk, at your hours, for your kind of passerby.”
- “The best wording on paper can fail when glare, distance, noise, or a busy counter enters the picture.”

**Use when:** preventing one-size-fits-all advice.

### Hook family G — The constraint advantage

**Idea:** Constraints can sharpen a message instead of ending the conversation.

**Openers:**

- “Limited space is not a copy problem until you have decided what the space must do.”
- “When you can only say one thing, choosing the right thing becomes the strategy.”

**Use when:** discussing small windows, narrow counters, limited staff time, or temporary setups.

### Hook family H — The respectful contrarian

**Idea:** Challenge a common habit with a clearer diagnostic question.

**Openers:**

- “More signs do not automatically create more clarity. Sometimes they create a harder reading job.”
- “A louder message is not always a stronger message. Start with whether the right person can understand it.”

**Use when:** correcting hype or default tactics without attacking the reader.

## 6. Lead-magnet framing

### Working asset

**Title:** *The 10-Minute Storefront & Counter Audit Checklist*

**Role:** A compact utility that lets a small-business owner inspect visible customer touchpoints in a short, repeatable pass.

### Responsible framing

> In ten minutes, walk the path a first-time visitor might take—from sidewalk or entrance to counter—and record what is easy to see, easy to understand, and easy to do next. This checklist does not predict sales. It gives you a structured way to notice friction before deciding what to change.

### What the lead magnet contains

- a start-point and route instruction;
- 15 observation prompts across storefront, entrance, and counter;
- a simple `clear / unclear / missing` mark for each prompt;
- space for one evidence note per item;
- a priority filter: `fix now / investigate / leave as is`;
- a final “one change, one observation” review box;
- a reminder to consider accessibility, local context, business hours, and operational constraints.

### Suggested sections

1. **From the sidewalk:** Can a new person identify the business and its category?
2. **At the entrance:** Is the next step apparent without staff intervention?
3. **At the counter:** Can the visitor understand the relevant choice or action?
4. **During the visit:** Does the physical message match the actual experience?
5. **After the pass:** What is one change worth inspecting, and what would count as an observation rather than a promised result?

### Lead-magnet copy rules

Use `audit`, `notice`, `record`, `clarify`, `prioritize`, and `inspect`. Do not use `fix your sales leak`, `guaranteed growth`, `instant conversion`, `proven checklist`, or quantified outcome promises. The value is clarity and a repeatable review process.

### Opt-in / delivery framing

Keep the invitation utility-led and non-commercial:

> Get the free 10-Minute Storefront & Counter Audit Checklist. Use it to inspect the first few customer moments in your space, note what is unclear, and choose one practical question to investigate next.

Avoid scarcity, pressure, income language, and claims that downloading the checklist will produce a business result.

## 7. Article and post architecture

### Standard article flow

1. **Opening hook:** A concrete customer moment or recurring hook.
2. **Problem definition:** Name the observable friction without blaming the owner.
3. **Why it matters:** Explain the decision or comprehension problem in plain language.
4. **Diagnostic checks:** Give a short inspection sequence.
5. **Practical options:** Offer bounded options and explain the trade-offs.
6. **Context note:** State what varies by space, audience, timing, or operations.
7. **Observation plan:** Tell the reader what to look at after making a change.
8. **Close:** Leave one useful next step and invite reflection, not a purchase.

### Short-form post flow

- one observable tension;
- one plain-language principle;
- three inspection prompts or one small exercise;
- one boundary against overclaiming;
- one low-pressure closing question.

### Evidence labeling inside copy

Use these labels when a draft includes research or interpretation:

- **What we can observe:** a visible or documented condition.
- **Working interpretation:** why that condition may create confusion or friction.
- **What to inspect next:** the reader’s own observation step.
- **What this does not prove:** the boundary against causal or outcome claims.

## 8. Opening templates

Templates are starting structures; rewrite them to fit the specific topic.

### Template A — Observation first

> Stand [where the customer would stand] and look at [touchpoint]. What can a first-time visitor understand in [brief moment]—and what still requires a guess? That gap is where I would start.

### Template B — Respectful diagnosis

> If [common condition] is happening in your space, it does not mean you are doing everything wrong. It means [specific customer moment] deserves a closer look. Here is the simple inspection I would use first.

### Template C — Contrarian clarity

> The instinct is to add [more text / more signs / more instructions]. But more information is not the same as more clarity. Before adding anything, check whether [diagnostic question].

### Template D — Constraint-led

> You do not need unlimited space, time, or staff attention to examine this. You need to decide what [touchpoint] must help a visitor understand, then remove whatever competes with that job.

### Template E — First-person authority

> When I review a storefront, I start with [observable check], not [common vanity metric or assumption]. It tells me whether the message is doing its first job: helping the right person understand what happens next.

### Template F — Audit invitation

> Take ten minutes and walk [route]. Mark each moment as [clear / unclear / missing]. Do not change anything yet. First collect the observations that will make the next decision less of a guess.

## 9. Closing templates

### Template A — One next step

> Before you change [touchpoint], inspect it from [customer position] at [relevant time]. Write down one thing that is clear, one thing that is unclear, and one question you still need to answer.

### Template B — Bounded action

> If you take one idea from this piece, make it this: [principle]. Try it as a small, observable change, then review what people notice—not what you hope the change will prove.

### Template C — Context reminder

> Your space, audience, hours, and operating constraints will shape the right version. Use this as a starting point, not a script. Keep the part that makes the customer’s next step easier to understand.

### Template D — Reflection question

> Walk the customer path once before you close for the day. Where did you hesitate, search, or need an explanation? That moment is a better starting point than another generic tactic.

### Template E — Authority sign-off

> Clear communication is not about saying everything. It is about helping the right person understand the next step at the right moment. Start there, and let your own observations guide the next change.

### Template F — Lead-magnet close

> If you want a structured pass through these questions, use the *10-Minute Storefront & Counter Audit Checklist*. It is a way to record observations and priorities—not a promise of a particular business outcome.
